Hours & Cost

How many hours will I need?

+

The FAA minimum is 40 hours, but let's be real: almost nobody in the Bay Area finishes in 40. Most of our students land around 70–80 hours, and some need more. It really comes down to your age, how quickly things click for you, how often you fly, and how much prep you do between lessons. And if you see a Bay Area school promising way fewer hours, be careful. A low number looks great on a website, but it's usually just there to get you in the door. We'd rather give you the honest number now than surprise you with the real bill later.

I've heard people get their private pilot certificate in 50–60 hours. Is that true?

+

Sure, it happens, usually at quiet airports out in the middle of nowhere. If that's the route you want, go for it. Here's the thing, though: training around SFO takes longer because every single flight is the real deal. Busy traffic, tower calls, complex airspace, all the stuff that trips up new pilots after they get their certificate. You'll face it here with an instructor sitting right next to you, not alone for the first time on some weekend trip. Those extra hours aren't padding. They're what make you sharp on the radio, aware of what's around you, and confident making the right call. Learn to fly here, and you can fly anywhere in the country.

Are the hours listed above guaranteed?

+

Nope, and honestly, nobody can guarantee that. Every student learns at their own pace. What we can give you is our best estimate, based on years of teaching all kinds of students, from teenagers to retirees. The goal is simple: give you a realistic picture of what training will cost, so you can plan ahead and not get caught off guard halfway through.

Are fuel costs included in the aircraft rental rate?

+

Yes. Sky Warriors' advertised rental rates include fuel, so students don't pay separately for normal training flights.

What's not included?

+

The FAA written test, checkride fee, medical exam, headset, and pilot supplies. These are listed under Additional Fees.

Payment & Financing

Do I have to pay upfront?

+

Not at all. You can pay as you go, one lesson at a time, or buy hours in blocks if that's easier for you. Either way, your money is never locked in. Any unused balance in your account is fully refundable, no questions asked. And if you buy a block over $5,000, we'll add an extra 1.5% in flying credit, so you get a little more time in the air for your money.

How long is each lesson, and how much does it cost?

+

Each lesson takes about 2–2.5 hours. That includes a short briefing before you fly, 1.5–2 hours in the air, and a debrief afterward where your instructor reviews how it went and what's next.

Here's what a typical lesson costs:

Cessna 152about $350–$450 per lesson
Cessna 172about $400–$535 per lesson

How we get those numbers: You pay for the airplane only while it's flying ($129/hr for the 152, $169/hr for the 172, fuel included). Your instructor is $85/hr for the flight plus about 20 minutes of briefing and debriefing. So a 1.5-hour flight in the 172, for example, comes to about $254 for the airplane plus $153 for the instructor, or roughly $407 total.

Is financing available?

+

Yes! We partner with Stratus Finance to help you fund your training. Checking your options is easy and only uses a soft credit pull, so it won't affect your credit score. Students with a credit score above 700 and a monthly income of around $3,000–$4,000 or more have a very strong chance of being approved. It's a quick way to find out what you qualify for before you commit to anything.

Aircraft

Cessna 152 or Cessna 172?

+

Both will get the student to the same goal. The 152 costs less and is a good fit for students under 190 lbs who want to keep costs as low as possible. The 172 is roomier, more comfortable, has more modern avionics, and is the more common training aircraft. For students who can spend a little more, it's worth it.

Schedule & Timeline

How long does it take?

+

It mostly depends on how often you fly. At 2–3 lessons a week, most students finish in about 4–6 months. If you can fly 5 days a week, you could be done in 2–3 months. The big secret is consistency: the more regularly you fly, the less time you spend reviewing and the faster everything clicks. But speed isn't the whole story. Our goal isn't just to get you in the air quickly. It's to make sure that when you're up there, you're a safe, confident pilot.

How often should I fly?

+

We recommend flying at least two to three times a week whenever you can. Flying consistently means less time relearning what you did last lesson, a lower overall cost, and a faster finish. Can you fly just once a week? Technically, yes, and if you don't mind a much longer timeline, it can work. But we don't recommend it. With a full week between lessons, skills fade fast, and you'll end up paying for extra hours just to get back to where you left off.

I live far away and work weekdays. Can I still train?

+

Absolutely. A lot of our students are in the same boat. For people who can't make it in during the week, we offer double lessons on Saturdays and Sundays. You'll fly one session in the morning, take a lunch break, then head back up for a second lesson in the afternoon. That way, you make the most of every trip in and still keep the consistency you need to progress steadily.

Requirements

Is there a minimum or maximum age?

+

There's no minimum age to begin training, and no maximum. The FAA requires students to be 16 to fly solo and 17 to earn a Private Pilot Certificate. As long as the student can get the appropriate FAA medical certificate and safely operate an aircraft, they can train. Our students range from teenagers to retirees.

When do I need a medical?

+

Before the student's first solo. We recommend getting it early so it doesn't hold up training.

Do I need to pass the FAA written test before I start flying?

+

Nope. You just need to pass it before your checkride. That said, we do recommend getting through at least 50% of the ground knowledge before you start flying, so you show up to your first lessons with a solid foundation. The rest is actually easier to learn along the way, because the ground knowledge makes way more sense once you've seen it in the airplane. 

Do I have to attend ground school?

+

Ground training is required, but you get to choose how you do it. You can learn one-on-one with an instructor, take an online course, study on your own, or mix and match whatever works best for you. To help you out, we also provide free study materials for the ground portion, so you're not starting from scratch.

Do I have to be a U.S. citizen?

+

No. U.S. citizens, green card holders, work authorization holders, and many visa holders can become pilots. Non-U.S. citizens must get approval through the TSA Flight Training Security Program (FTSP) before starting, which includes fingerprinting and usually takes 1–2 weeks.

Will my hours transfer if I switch schools, even in another state?

+

Yes. A student's logged flight hours belong to them and count at any flight school in the country.

How to spend less

Six ways to save on flight training

01

Fly more often, not less

Long gaps between lessons mean re-learning what you already paid to learn. Students who fly three or more times a week finish in fewer total hours, which is the single biggest cost lever you control.

02

Show up prepared

Study the maneuvers and procedures before the lesson, not during it. Every minute your instructor spends teaching something you could have read is billed at the instruction rate.

03

Use the cheaper airframe

If you are on a very tight budget and weigh under 200 lb, train in the Cessna 152 at $129 an hour instead of a $169 172. Same certificate, and on a 70-hour program that difference is roughly $2,800.

04

Use our flight simulator

Learn procedures, navigation and instrument familiarization in the sim instead of the aircraft. It builds the same understanding at a fraction of the hourly cost, with no fuel burn and no weather delays.

05

Brief before, debrief after

Take the briefing seriously before you fly and talk the flight through once the aircraft is tied down. Questions answered on the ground cost a fraction of the same questions in the air.

06

Write it down

Keep notes after every lesson so you remember what to work on, and target your repeated mistakes before the next flight.
